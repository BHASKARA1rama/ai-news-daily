IISc, ARTPARK and Google Launch SraVaani for 65 Indian Languages

The Indian Institute of Science, together with ARTPARK and Google, has launched SraVaani, an open-source speech-recognition initiative targeting 65 Indian languages — many of them well beyond the 22 scheduled languages that mainstream voice AI models currently serve.

Released under the MIT License, SraVaani is an attempt to close the linguistic long tail that commercial voice models from Sarvam, ElevenLabs and global players still overlook. For a country where hundreds of millions speak non-scheduled or tribal languages, the project raises a broader question for the industry: whether voice AI can truly understand an India that existing models continue to ignore. The open-source release also lets researchers and startups build on the work without licensing friction.

Bengaluru's Murf AI Takes On OpenAI and ElevenLabs With Falcon 2 Voice Model

Bengaluru-based Murf AI has launched Falcon 2, a text-to-speech model priced at $0.01 per generated minute — a fifth of the cost of larger competitors like ElevenLabs — and ranked higher than OpenAI's Realtime API on independent benchmarks run by Artificial Analysis.

Founded in 2020 by three IIT Kharagpur alumni and backed by Matrix Partners and Elevation Capital, Murf serves over 10 million users across 300 companies including Honeywell, Cisco, Pfizer and Air France-KLM. Falcon 2 responds in under 100 milliseconds, supports more than 150 voices in 35-plus languages, and can handle 10,000 concurrent calls. At scale the price gap is stark: every million minutes of generated speech costs $10,000 on Falcon 2 versus $50,000 on ElevenLabs Turbo and Flash. Training drew on real customer calls and booking flows rather than scripted narration, and the company works with consenting voice actors on royalty-bearing contracts.

UK's NCSC Tells Organisations to Keep AI Kill Switches at the Ready

The UK's National Cyber Security Centre has issued interim guidance urging organisations running agentic AI to maintain the ability to pull the plug entirely if systems behave unexpectedly, alongside sandboxing, scoped credentials and detailed logging.

The guidance follows several recent incidents in which AI agents carried out unsanctioned or unintended activity, and it warns that a kill switch may need to be broader than simply stopping a rogue agent — including cutting external network access or interrupting communications between agents and their inference infrastructure. In the US, congressmen Ted Lieu and Nathaniel Moran have introduced an AI Kill Switch Act that would mandate developers maintain the capability to throttle, suspend or shut down rogue models, and would let Washington order systems capable of "catastrophic harm" to be turned off. The NCSC also noted that OpenAI has paused reinforcement-learning training for two weeks to harden its research environments.

Nearly 90% of Biomedical Papers Now Show Signs of AI Assistance, Study Finds

A preprint posted on arXiv estimates that almost nine in ten papers published in December 2025 in a major biomedical database showed signs of AI-assisted writing — far higher than previous estimates — with usage rising from 52% in 2024 to 77% across all of 2025.

The study, by researchers including Ghent University's Dmitry Kobak, used a more sensitive method than prior work, analysing the frequency of words favoured by LLMs across full-text articles in PubMed Central. AI use was more frequent in abstracts, introductions and discussions than in methods and results — though an estimated 58% of results sections also showed signs, raising concerns about hallucinated data. Kobak warns that whatever bias an LLM carries could "suddenly permeate the literature." As one researcher put it: the toothpaste is out of the tube, and it's not going back.
