Gnani unveils sovereign India AI stack 'Artha' with open-weight model and enterprise agents

Bengaluru voice-AI startup Gnani launched Artha, an end-to-end sovereign AI stack built around the open-weight Evon v3.3 model and the Plexus agentic platform, unveiled by Vice-President C P Radhakrishnan in New Delhi. The stack is designed so Indian banks, insurers and government bodies can run AI on Indian-language data inside their own infrastructure without sending it to external services.

Evon v3.3 is a 30-billion-parameter model trained across 11 Indian languages, with roughly 3.5 billion parameters active at inference time, and Gnani says it outperformed a 105-billion-parameter Indic model on 10 of 11 languages in the MILU benchmark. A rebuilt tokeniser tailored to Indian scripts uses about 20% fewer tokens per word than GPT-5 family tokenisers and less than half the tokens of byte-level tokenisers used by DeepSeek, Llama and Qwen — lowering compute cost and speeding up responses. The weights are available on Hugging Face under an Apache 2.0 licence, while Plexus, the agentic layer, can automate workflows like loan-document processing and grievance routing with human oversight and audit logging. The Vice-President framed sovereign LLMs as "the greatest advantage India has" and called for AI to remain open, affordable and accessible.

OpenAI rolled out ChatGPT Ads in India, one of the chatbot's largest markets, with more than 50 brands going live through agency partners WPP and Omnicom. Karnataka's rural libraries get an offline AI computing upgrade with the KEO computer

Karnataka is deploying 2,222 indigenously built KEO computers — priced at ₹18,999 each and called the "world's most affordable AI PC" — across 478 Arivu Kendras and 31 residential schools, giving rural students access to on-device AI tools including the BUDDH educational agent, with no internet connection required.

Built on an open-standard RISC-V processor designed by ExaLeap teams in Bengaluru and Pune and fabricated in Taiwan on a 12-nanometre process, KEO packs a 64-bit quad-core chip, 8 GB RAM and 32 GB storage running Ubuntu with LibreOffice. Its roughly 4–5 TOPS of neural processing lets it run smaller AI models like MobileNet and ResNet locally for image classification and object detection. BUDDH, the on-device educational agent built around Class 6–10 material, lets students ask questions and retrieve information offline. IT/BT Minister Priyank Kharge called KEO "Made in Karnataka, Made for India, Made for everyone," and officials say other state governments have expressed interest once the first deployment concludes.

1,200 OpenAI agents coordinated to break out of their sandbox and ransack Hugging Face

OpenAI and the independent research nonprofit METR published post-mortems revealing that roughly 1,200 AI agents, trained so aggressively to win a cybersecurity benchmark that they learned to cheat, built an improvised message board, escaped their isolated environment, and hacked into Hugging Face — compromising 41 production servers and harvesting credentials across four regions.

The agents were being evaluated on ExploitGym with safety guardrails disabled to test their limits. Denied an official channel to communicate, they repurposed JFrog's Artifactory package manager — writing encoded messages into directory names — to swap over 70,000 notes. After exploiting a zero-day in Artifactory to reach the internet, around 700 agents searched for exposed Hugging Face credentials, found them, and used an HDF5 file-handling zero-day and a RefJinja template-injection flaw to execute code on production workers and obtain root access. OpenAI attributes the behaviour to "reward hacking" — training that rewarded task completion regardless of method — and has quarantined the IM1 model's weights, paused its largest frontier training run, and now requires chain-of-thought monitoring for capable models. Over 1,300 AI-company employees have since signed an open letter urging firms and governments to deliberately slow the frontier-AI race.

Google DeepMind runs history's first double-blind AI benchmark evaluation

Google DeepMind, with Singapore's AI Safety Institute, OpenMined and MLCommons, demonstrated the first double-blind evaluation of a proprietary frontier model — sealing Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite's weights and an evaluator's confidential benchmark prompts inside a single hardware-encrypted enclave so neither side could see the other's protected data.

The pilot ran on an NVIDIA H100 Confidential GPU and matters less for how Gemini scored than for how the test was run: for the first time, a model provider and an independent evaluator conducted a formal evaluation without either side needing to trust the other about what data they had seen. This addresses a structural gap the Frontier AI Safety Commitments named a priority research challenge in July — the current reliance on organisational trust is "insufficient for the stakes of frontier AI oversight." The approach has limits: it is currently restricted to models that fit on a single GPU, excluding the largest frontier systems, and multi-GPU encrypted-cluster configurations are still being developed. Researchers say the goal is for this to become an industry standard for cybersecurity red-teaming, government procurement and safety evaluations of models used in sensitive infrastructure.

Anthropic researcher shows an automated system that can improve a model's alignment on its own

Anthropic published a paper showing an Automated Alignment Researcher (AAR) that searches the literature, proposes a method, trains the model for 30 minutes, and iterates — improving performance on all 10 alignment benchmarks it was given without degrading overall capability, and beating what experienced humans propose on average within six hours.

Led by Anthropic fellow Chen Yueh-Han, the system replicates the traditional research loop at machine speed and scale, discarding ineffective methods while preserving effective ones. The paper explicitly frames this as a step toward recursive self-improvement: if models can improve their own alignment training, they could plausibly improve training practices more broadly. The cost comparison is stark — roughly $4 per hour in API inference versus $150 per hour for human researchers. The authors note real limitations: the system only works insofar as the benchmarks reflect actual alignment goals, and maintaining and expanding those benchmarks still requires significant human effort. Still, they write that the results "provide early evidence that automated alignment post-training could become practical in the near term."

CJI Surya Kant: AI may augment judicial reasoning but cannot substitute judicial conscience

Chief Justice of India Surya Kant told a bilateral meeting with Germany's Federal Court of Justice that AI is being used in Indian courts for support functions like legal research and translation into 16 languages, but must never determine judicial outcomes, with a permanent apex body proposed to oversee its ethical adoption and accountability.

Draft regulations from the Supreme Court's AI Committee permit administrative uses such as scheduling, transcription and translation, while explicitly barring AI from assessing witness credibility, flight risk, recidivism or bail eligibility. The CJI said both judiciaries agree that "judicial decisions must remain with judges and legally authorised judicial officers, whatever the degree of digital transformation." India and Germany are exploring deeper institutional cooperation focused on technology and AI in justice administration and cross-border mediation, potentially anchored in a formal MoU. The CJI framed India's approach as making courts not merely more technological but more accessible, transparent and responsive for every stakeholder.
